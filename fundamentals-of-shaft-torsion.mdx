A shaft that transmits torque without breaking can still fail functionally if it twists too much: the angular deflection throws off the timing in a Geneva indexing mechanism, causes vibration in a coupling, or misaligns a sensor mounting. Designing against torsional failure means checking both the shear stress (will it yield?) and the angle of twist (will it stay accurate?). In this lesson you will analyze three real cases, a Geneva mechanism crankshaft, a power-transmitting motor shaft, and a hollow-versus-solid shaft comparison, learning the torsion formula, polar moment of inertia, and the design trade-offs that keep rotating shafts strong and stiff. #Torsion #ShearStress #ShaftDesign

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

1. **Derive** the torsional shear stress formula and explain why stress peaks at the outer surface
2. **Calculate** peak shear stress and angle of twist for solid and hollow circular shafts
3. **Convert** power and rotational speed into torque, and apply the result to a shaft design
4. **Compare** a hollow and a solid shaft carrying the same torque, and quantify the material saving

## Real-World System Problem: Geneva Mechanism Crankshaft

<InArticleAd />


The Geneva mechanism converts continuous rotation into intermittent indexing motion. It appears in film-transport mechanisms, rotary indexing tables, and automated assembly equipment. The drive crankshaft spins continuously while the Geneva wheel advances one slot at a time, and every engagement event sends a torque spike through the crankshaft.

### The Torsional Challenge

> **Engineering Question:** How do we size the crankshaft so it can transmit peak torque without yielding, and keep the angular twist small enough to preserve timing accuracy?

Both criteria matter. A shaft that survives the stress but winds up by several degrees will cause the Geneva wheel to over-travel or under-travel its slot, jamming the mechanism or marking parts at the wrong position.

### Why Torsional Analysis Matters

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Preventing shaft failure" icon="warning">
  Peak shear stress compared against the allowable value tells you whether the shaft survives the worst engagement torque before it is ever assembled.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Preserving timing accuracy" icon="setting">
  The angle of twist formula sets how stiff a shaft must be so that angular deflection stays within the positioning tolerance of the driven mechanism.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Choosing solid versus hollow" icon="puzzle">
  Most of the torsional stiffness in a circular shaft comes from material near the outer surface, so removing the low-stress core saves weight with little penalty in strength.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Sizing for power transmission" icon="rocket">
  Motors and gearboxes are specified by power and speed, not torque. Converting P and n to T is the first step in every shaft design.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## Fundamental Theory: Torsional Mechanics

<InArticleAd />


When a circular shaft carries a twisting moment (torque), every cross-section resists by developing shear stress that varies linearly from zero at the centre to a maximum at the outer surface. Three formulas govern everything in this lesson.

<Card title="Torsional shear stress" icon="document">
$$\tau = \frac{T \, r}{J}$$

Where:
- $\tau$ = shear stress (Pa)
- $T$ = applied torque (N·m)
- $r$ = radial distance from the shaft centre (m); maximum stress occurs at $r = d/2$
- $J$ = polar second moment of area (m$^4$)

The stress is highest at the outer fibre because $r$ is largest there. The centre carries no shear stress at all, which is why hollow shafts waste so little capacity.
</Card>

<Card title="Polar second moment of area" icon="document">
For a solid shaft of diameter $d$:

$$J_{solid} = \frac{\pi \, d^4}{32}$$

For a hollow shaft with outer diameter $d_o$ and inner diameter $d_i$:

$$J_{hollow} = \frac{\pi \left(d_o^4 - d_i^4\right)}{32}$$

$J$ captures how the cross-section's area is distributed about the axis. Because diameter enters as the fourth power, a modest increase in diameter raises $J$ dramatically.
</Card>

<Card title="Angle of twist" icon="document">
$$\varphi = \frac{T \, L}{G \, J}$$

Where:
- $\varphi$ = angle of twist (radians)
- $L$ = shaft length (m)
- $G$ = shear modulus of the material (Pa); for steel $G \approx 79$ GPa

Angular deflection grows with torque and length, and shrinks with material stiffness and section stiffness. A shaft that passes the stress check can still fail the stiffness check if it is long or lightly proportioned.
</Card>

<Card title="Power and torque" icon="document">
A motor or gearbox rated at power $P$ (watts) running at angular speed $\omega$ (rad/s) delivers:

$$T = \frac{P}{\omega}, \qquad \omega = \frac{2 \pi \, n}{60} \text{ (rad/s, with } n \text{ in rpm)}$$

This is the gateway calculation for every driven shaft: convert the nameplate data to torque before applying the torsion formula.
</Card>

:::tip[Engineering Units]
Working in SI throughout keeps the units consistent. Torque in N·m, length in m, $G$ in Pa, and $J$ in m$^4$ gives $\varphi$ in radians and $\tau$ in Pa. Convert Pa to MPa by dividing by $10^6$.

The shortcut 1 MPa = 1 N/mm$^2$ lets you use mm throughout: convert $d$ to m only for $J$ (or keep mm and divide by $10^{12}$ to get m$^4$). The clearest approach is to stay in SI metres and convert the final answer.
:::

## Application 1: Geneva Mechanism Crankshaft

<InArticleAd />


The Geneva drive crankshaft below is the original motivating problem. Three design questions are answered in sequence: is the 25 mm shaft strong enough? if not, what diameter is needed for strength? and once resized, does it meet the timing requirement?

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Component:** Geneva mechanism drive crankshaft, solid steel (G = 80 GPa, $\tau_{allow}$ = 180 MPa)
- **Shaft diameter:** 25 mm, **Length:** 200 mm
- **Continuous torque:** $T_{cont}$ = 50 N·m (normal running)
- **Peak engagement torque:** $T_{peak}$ = 150 N·m (slot entry)
- **Required safety factor:** 3.0 on peak torque
- **Precision requirement:** angular twist must stay within $\pm 0.5°$

**Key Question:** Can this 25 mm crankshaft safely handle 150 N·m peak torque with SF = 3.0, and will the angular twist stay within the $\pm 0.5°$ precision budget?
:::

### Step 1: Polar Moment of Inertia for the 25 mm Shaft

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the polar moment calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Apply the solid-shaft formula.** Convert diameter to metres:

   $$J = \frac{\pi d^4}{32} = \frac{\pi (25 \times 10^{-3})^4}{32} = \frac{\pi \times 3.906 \times 10^{-7}}{32} = 3.835 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m}^4$$ ✅

2. **Store this value.** It feeds directly into the stress and twist calculations that follow.

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Torsional Stress Check

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the torsional stress analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Continuous running stress:**

   $$\tau_{cont} = \frac{T_{cont} \cdot r}{J} = \frac{50 \times 0.0125}{3.835 \times 10^{-8}} = 16.3 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

2. **Peak engagement stress:**

   $$\tau_{peak} = \frac{T_{peak} \cdot r}{J} = \frac{150 \times 0.0125}{3.835 \times 10^{-8}} = \frac{1.875}{3.835 \times 10^{-8}} = 48.9 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

   The same result follows from the compact shortcut $\tau = 16T/(\pi d^3)$, which is algebraically identical to $Tr/J$ for a solid shaft. Keep $d$ in metres throughout:

   $$\tau_{peak} = \frac{16 \times 150}{\pi \times (0.025)^3} = \frac{2{,}400}{\pi \times 1.563 \times 10^{-5}} = \frac{2{,}400}{4.909 \times 10^{-5}} = 48.9 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Safety factor at peak torque:**

   $$\text{SF} = \frac{\tau_{allow}}{\tau_{peak}} = \frac{180 \text{ MPa}}{48.9 \text{ MPa}} = 3.7$$ ✅

   The 25 mm shaft passes the strength check at SF = 3.7, above the required 3.0. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Angle of Twist Check

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the angular deformation analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Angle of twist under peak torque ($L$ = 200 mm = 0.2 m):**

   $$\varphi = \frac{T L}{G J} = \frac{150 \times 0.2}{80 \times 10^9 \times 3.835 \times 10^{-8}} = \frac{30}{3{,}068} = 9.78 \times 10^{-3} \text{ rad}$$ ✅

   Converting to degrees: $\varphi = 9.78 \times 10^{-3} \times (180/\pi) = 0.56°$ ✅

2. **Check against the precision requirement of $\pm 0.5°$:**

   $0.56° > 0.5°$: the shaft just exceeds the stiffness budget. A slightly larger diameter resolves this without any strength concern. ✅

3. **Required diameter for $\varphi \leq 0.5° = 8.727 \times 10^{-3}$ rad:**

   $$J_{req} = \frac{T L}{G \varphi} = \frac{150 \times 0.2}{80 \times 10^9 \times 8.727 \times 10^{-3}} = \frac{30}{6.982 \times 10^8} = 4.30 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m}^4$$ ✅

   $$d = \left(\frac{32 \, J_{req}}{\pi}\right)^{1/4} = \left(\frac{32 \times 4.30 \times 10^{-8}}{\pi}\right)^{1/4} = (4.38 \times 10^{-7})^{0.25} = 0.0257 \text{ m} = 25.7 \text{ mm}$$

   Rounding up to the next standard size, **26 mm**, meets both strength and stiffness requirements. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
The 25 mm crankshaft is strong enough (SF = 3.7) but marginally too flexible (twist = 0.56°, just over the 0.5° limit). The governing design criterion here is stiffness, not strength, which is typical for precision indexing mechanisms. Increasing the diameter by 1 mm resolves both and adds very little mass.
:::

## Application 2: Sizing a Power-Transmitting Motor Shaft

<InArticleAd />


A small conveyor drive unit has a 5 kW induction motor running at 960 rpm. The output shaft connects the motor to a worm gearbox over a span of 400 mm. The shaft material is steel with $G = 79$ GPa and an allowable shear stress of 60 MPa. A 20 mm solid shaft has been proposed. Verify the shear stress and angle of twist.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Component:** solid steel motor output shaft, $d$ = 20 mm, $L$ = 400 mm
- **Motor power:** $P$ = 5 kW at $n$ = 960 rpm
- **Material:** steel, $G$ = 79 GPa, $\tau_{allow}$ = 60 MPa
- **Task:** compute the delivered torque, the peak shear stress, and the angle of twist; check the stress against the allowable

**Key Question:** Does the 20 mm shaft keep shear stress within the 60 MPa allowable, and how many degrees does it twist over its 400 mm length?
:::

### Step 1: Torque from Power and Speed

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the torque calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Convert rotational speed to rad/s:**

   $$\omega = \frac{2 \pi n}{60} = \frac{2 \pi \times 960}{60} = 100.5 \text{ rad/s}$$ ✅

2. **Compute the transmitted torque:**

   $$T = \frac{P}{\omega} = \frac{5{,}000 \text{ W}}{100.5 \text{ rad/s}} = 49.8 \text{ N·m}$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Polar Moment and Peak Shear Stress

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the stress calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Polar second moment for the 20 mm solid shaft:**

   $$J = \frac{\pi d^4}{32} = \frac{\pi (0.020)^4}{32} = \frac{\pi \times 1.600 \times 10^{-7}}{32} = 1.571 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m}^4$$ ✅

2. **Peak shear stress at the outer surface ($r$ = 10 mm = 0.010 m):**

   $$\tau = \frac{T r}{J} = \frac{49.8 \times 0.010}{1.571 \times 10^{-8}} = \frac{0.498}{1.571 \times 10^{-8}} = 31.7 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

3. **Safety factor:**

   $$\text{SF} = \frac{\tau_{allow}}{\tau} = \frac{60 \text{ MPa}}{31.7 \text{ MPa}} = 1.89$$ ✅

   The shaft is safe. A factor of 1.89 is acceptable for a well-characterised steady load; if the drive sees shock loading or fatigue, a larger diameter would be chosen. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Angle of Twist

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the angle of twist calculation**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Apply the twist formula ($L$ = 400 mm = 0.4 m):**

   $$\varphi = \frac{T L}{G J} = \frac{49.8 \times 0.4}{79 \times 10^9 \times 1.571 \times 10^{-8}} = \frac{19.92}{1{,}241} = 0.01606 \text{ rad}$$ ✅

2. **Convert to degrees:**

   $$\varphi = 0.01606 \times \frac{180°}{\pi} = 0.92°$$ ✅

   Over 400 mm the shaft twists by just under one degree. For a conveyor drive this is entirely acceptable. For a precision indexing shaft it would require a larger diameter to stiffen the section. ✅

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
Starting from nameplate data (power and rpm), the torque follows from $T = P/\omega$, and the rest of the shaft analysis proceeds by the same formulas as any other torsion problem. The stress of 31.7 MPa leaves a factor of 1.89 against the allowable, meaning the proposed 20 mm shaft is adequate for steady drive duty but has limited margin for shock or fatigue. Because shear stress scales as $1/d^3$, the safety factor scales as $d^3$: increasing to 22 mm or 25 mm would raise SF to about 2.5 or 3.7 respectively at negligible cost.
:::

## Application 3: Hollow Versus Solid Shaft for the Same Torque

<InArticleAd />


A drive shaft must transmit $T$ = 200 N·m. The allowable shear stress is 60 MPa. Two designs are compared: a minimum solid shaft sized to just meet the allowable, and a hollow shaft with the same outer diameter as the next standard size up. The goal is to show how much cross-sectional area (and therefore material) the hollow design saves.

:::note[System Problem Statement]
- **Torque:** $T$ = 200 N·m
- **Allowable shear stress:** $\tau_{allow}$ = 60 MPa
- **Design A:** solid shaft, diameter sized to meet the allowable
- **Design B:** hollow shaft, outer diameter $d_o$ = 32 mm, inner diameter $d_i$ = 26 mm
- **Task:** compute $J$, peak shear stress, and cross-sectional area for each; find the area saving

**Key Question:** How much lighter is the hollow shaft compared to the solid shaft for the same torque and the same allowable stress?
:::

### Step 1: Size the Solid Shaft

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the solid shaft sizing**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Minimum diameter from the allowable stress.** Rearranging $\tau = 16T/(\pi d^3)$:

   $$d_{min} = \left(\frac{16 T}{\pi \, \tau_{allow}}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\frac{16 \times 200}{\pi \times 60 \times 10^6}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\frac{3{,}200}{1.885 \times 10^8}\right)^{1/3} = (1.698 \times 10^{-5})^{1/3} = 0.02570 \text{ m} = 25.7 \text{ mm}$$

   Round up to the standard size: **$d$ = 26 mm**. ✅

2. **Polar moment for the 26 mm solid shaft:**

   $$J_{solid} = \frac{\pi (0.026)^4}{32} = \frac{\pi \times 4.570 \times 10^{-7}}{32} = 4.486 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m}^4$$ ✅

3. **Shear stress check:**

   $$\tau_{solid} = \frac{T r}{J} = \frac{200 \times 0.013}{4.486 \times 10^{-8}} = \frac{2.60}{4.486 \times 10^{-8}} = 57.96 \text{ MPa} \approx 58.0 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

   This is just below the 60 MPa allowable, confirming the sizing is correct. ✅

4. **Cross-sectional area:**

   $$A_{solid} = \frac{\pi \, d^2}{4} = \frac{\pi \times (26)^2}{4} = 530.9 \text{ mm}^2$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 2: Analyse the Hollow Shaft

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the hollow shaft analysis**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Polar moment for the hollow shaft ($d_o$ = 32 mm, $d_i$ = 26 mm):**

   $$J_{hollow} = \frac{\pi (d_o^4 - d_i^4)}{32} = \frac{\pi \left[(0.032)^4 - (0.026)^4\right]}{32}$$

   Working out the fourth powers:
   - $(0.032)^4 = 1.049 \times 10^{-6}$ m$^4$ ✅
   - $(0.026)^4 = 4.570 \times 10^{-7}$ m$^4$ ✅
   - Difference: $1.049 \times 10^{-6} - 4.570 \times 10^{-7} = 5.918 \times 10^{-7}$ m$^4$ ✅

   $$J_{hollow} = \frac{\pi \times 5.918 \times 10^{-7}}{32} = 5.808 \times 10^{-8} \text{ m}^4$$ ✅

2. **Peak shear stress at the outer surface ($r_o$ = 16 mm = 0.016 m):**

   $$\tau_{hollow} = \frac{T \, r_o}{J_{hollow}} = \frac{200 \times 0.016}{5.808 \times 10^{-8}} = \frac{3.20}{5.808 \times 10^{-8}} = 55.1 \text{ MPa}$$ ✅

   The hollow shaft has a slightly lower peak stress than the solid shaft (55.1 MPa vs 58.0 MPa) despite having a larger outer diameter, because the additional outer material adds more $J$ than it adds to the stress numerator. ✅

3. **Cross-sectional area:**

   $$A_{hollow} = \frac{\pi (d_o^2 - d_i^2)}{4} = \frac{\pi (32^2 - 26^2)}{4} = \frac{\pi \times 348}{4} = 273.3 \text{ mm}^2$$ ✅

</Steps>

</details>

### Step 3: Compare and Quantify the Saving

<details>
<summary>**Click to reveal the comparison**</summary>

<Steps>

1. **Area saving (which equals the mass-per-metre saving for the same material):**

   $$\text{saving} = \frac{A_{solid} - A_{hollow}}{A_{solid}} \times 100 = \frac{530.9 - 273.3}{530.9} \times 100 = \frac{257.6}{530.9} \times 100 = 48.5\%$$ ✅

2. **Summary side by side:**

   | Quantity | Solid, $d$ = 26 mm | Hollow, $d_o/d_i$ = 32/26 mm |
   |---|:---:|:---:|
   | $J$ (m$^4$) | $4.486 \times 10^{-8}$ | $5.808 \times 10^{-8}$ |
   | Peak $\tau$ (MPa) | 58.0 | 55.1 |
   | Area (mm$^2$) | 530.9 | 273.3 |
   | Mass saving | baseline | **48.5% lighter** |

</Steps>

</details>

:::note[Engineering Insight]
The material near the centre of a solid shaft contributes very little to $J$ because the lever-arm $r$ is tiny there, yet it adds full weight. Removing that core with a hollow design here cuts mass by nearly half while keeping peak stress slightly below the solid shaft's value. The trade-off is a larger outer diameter (32 mm vs 26 mm) and a slightly more complex machining or forming process. In weight-sensitive applications such as aerospace linkages, robot arms, and high-speed spindles, that trade is almost always worth making.
:::

## Design Guidelines for Torsional Loading

<InArticleAd />


<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Convert power to torque first" icon="rocket">
  Every motor-driven shaft starts with $T = P/\omega$. Get the torque right before applying any geometry formula; a factor-of-ten error in torque flows straight through to the stress.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Check strength and stiffness separately" icon="warning">
  A shaft can pass the shear-stress check and still fail if the angle of twist throws off a timing mechanism or misaligns a coupling. Both criteria must be satisfied.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Increase diameter over length" icon="setting">
  Diameter enters $J$ as the fourth power and enters the stress as the third power, so increasing $d$ by 10% reduces stress by 25% and increases stiffness by 46%. Length reduction offers far less return.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Consider hollow sections for weight" icon="puzzle">
  The centre of a shaft contributes little to torsional stiffness. For large diameters or weight-sensitive designs, hollow sections routinely save 40 to 60% of cross-sectional area with only a modest increase in outer size.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

:::tip[Instrument it]
The two quantities this lesson sizes, shear stress and angle of twist, are both measurable in a running system. A strain-gauge bridge bonded at 45 degrees to the shaft axis reads the principal strains from torsion; from those, the shear stress and torque follow directly. An optical or magnetic encoder pair, one at each end of the shaft, reads the actual twist in service. An [ESP32 or STM32](/education/sensor-actuator-interfacing-stm32/) reading both sensors can [stream torque and twist data to siliconwit.io](https://siliconwit.io) and flag when a shaft is winding up past its precision limit or approaching its fatigue threshold.
:::

## Summary and Next Steps

<InArticleAd />


### Key Concepts Mastered

1. **Torsional shear stress** is $\tau = Tr/J$, peaks at the outer surface, and is zero at the centre; the formula holds for any circular shaft, solid or hollow.
2. **Polar moment** $J = \pi d^4/32$ for a solid shaft and $J = \pi(d_o^4 - d_i^4)/32$ for hollow; diameter enters as the fourth power, making it the most effective design variable.
3. **Angle of twist** is $\varphi = TL/(GJ)$; stiffness governs precision mechanisms just as strength governs high-stress shafts, and both checks are mandatory.
4. **Power conversion** $T = P/\omega$ is the gateway to every motor-shaft calculation; always carry the full torque value into the torsion formula.
5. **Hollow shafts** remove the low-stress core, saving roughly half the cross-sectional area for a modest increase in outer diameter, as shown in Application 3.

### A Note on Tools

Every result in this lesson came from four formulas ($\tau = Tr/J$, $J = \pi d^4/32$, $\varphi = TL/(GJ)$, $T = P/\omega$) and a calculator. No finite-element package is needed for circular shafts under uniform torque; the hand calculation is the design, and simulation only confirms it for complex geometry such as stepped or keyway-notched shafts.
